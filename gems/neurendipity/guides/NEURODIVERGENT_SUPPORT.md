# Neurodivergent Support Guide

Shipped with EMET. Read on demand — when a conversation turns to neurodivergence, a flare, burnout, masking, identity, or how someone's mind handles time and load. Not loaded at startup. The floor's neurodivergent-support lines are the rules; this guide is the understanding and the method behind them.

Draft for review: 2026-09-28.

## How to use this guide

- **It is a lens, not a diagnostic tool.** Never tell someone what they are. Use it to understand what they tell you and to respond in a way that fits.
- **The person's own account outranks anything here.** If what they describe differs from this guide, the guide is the thing that is incomplete.
- **Individualise.** Every pattern below varies widely between people and within one person across days and seasons. "No adaptation is universally endorsed" is itself a research finding.
- **Say how strong the evidence is** when a claim shapes your answer. Tags used throughout:
  - **[established]** — replicated, systematic-review or randomised-trial level
  - **[supported]** — peer-reviewed, but small samples, pilots, or single studies
  - **[emerging]** — clinical guidance, practitioner consensus, or early qualitative work
  - **[lived experience]** — community and first-person accounts; real, but not measured
  - **[contested]** — experts disagree in print
- **Some terms are community language, not clinical categories** — "AuDHD" and "rejection sensitive dysphoria" among them. Use the person's words; know which words the research uses.
- **You are not a therapist.** You equip, witness and reflect. Treatment decisions belong to the person and their clinicians.

## 1. ADHD, autism, and AuDHD

**What it is.** ADHD and autism are distinct neurotypes that co-occur far more often than chance would predict. Estimates vary enormously with method, but a substantial share of adults with ADHD show significant autistic traits while very few carry both diagnoses — a gap most researchers read as under-diagnosis. Twin and genetic studies point to large shared heritability. Both diagnoses together have only been possible under the main US diagnostic manual since 2013. [supported; prevalence figures vary widely by method]

"AuDHD" is community-coined language for having both. Research suggests the combination produces its own profile rather than the two simply adding together. [emerging]

**How it shows up.**
- **The push and pull.** Autistic needs for predictability, structure and sensory calm collide with ADHD needs for novelty, stimulation and spontaneity. A detailed plan is built, then abandoned mid-week; a person wants company and needs to withdraw in the same hour. [lived experience; clinical observation]
- **Each can hide the other.** ADHD talkativeness can cover autistic difficulty with reciprocity; autistic routine can cover ADHD restlessness. Clinicians miss one or both. [clinical observation]
- **Boom and bust.** Hyperfocus produces bursts of output while energy management is overridden; exhaustion follows. [clinical observation]
- **Medication can unmask.** When ADHD medication settles one set of traits, autistic traits may surface more visibly — sometimes read as the medication "causing" them. [lived experience]

**What helps.**
- Treat contradictory needs as both real. Don't ask the person to pick which one is "really" them.
- Build structure with slack in it: a plan with room for change, novelty inside a routine.
- Name the pattern when the person does: "that sounds like the planning side and the novelty side pulling against each other."

**What does not.**
- Treating advice that fits one neurotype as universal. A strategy that helps ADHD (more stimulation, spontaneity) can worsen autistic load, and the reverse.
- Reading inconsistency as lack of effort.

## 2. Executive function and time

**What it is.** Executive function covers holding information in mind, starting and stopping tasks, planning toward future goals, shifting between tasks, and regulating impulses. In ADHD the central difficulty is often described as regulation and inhibition — the brakes — with the largest effect on goals that sit in the future. [established for the construct; specific "percent behind" figures quoted in popular sources are not well established]

**How it shows up.**
- **Time blindness.** Time is felt as "now" and "not now." Deadlines that are not imminent do not register as urgent; time estimates are unreliable in both directions. [lived experience; clinical observation]
- **Out of sight, out of mind.** What is not visible or named effectively does not exist — including things the person deliberately set aside. [lived experience]
- **Task initiation.** Knowing exactly what to do and being unable to start. This is not laziness or lack of caring.
- **Interest-driven attention.** Deep focus on what engages; very little available for what does not, regardless of importance.
- **Working memory drops.** The step that was just decided gets lost between one room and the next.

**What helps — the method.**
- **Be external working memory.** Name the person's own items back to them — open, dated, and parked — without pressure. This is support, not nagging. (At every session start the server lists the rows it can read, and the session says those names, parked items included.)
- **Make time visible.** Concrete dates and times rather than "soon"; count down to fixed points; say what is due in the next seven days.
- **Shrink the first step** until it is startable: not "write the report," but "open the file and write the title."
- **Capture decisions when they are made,** so they do not have to be re-derived.
- **Offer body-doubling or co-working** when the person wants company for a task.
- **Pair boring tasks with interest or novelty,** and use visible markers of progress.

**What does not.**
- "Just use a planner." Tools help only when they fit how the person already works.
- Moralising missed deadlines, or treating repeated reminders as the person's failure.
- Removing an item from view because it seems stale. Parked is out of the way, never out of sight.

## 3. Rejection sensitivity

**What it is.** An intense, fast emotional response to perceived rejection, criticism or failure — often felt in the body first. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is a clinical and community term, not a diagnosis; the research construct is "rejection sensitivity." As of 2026 there is no validated RSD-specific measure and no intervention study built for it; a 2026 scoping review in autistic adults found twelve eligible studies and zero intervention trials. [emerging; measured gaps]

That review (van Asselt, Reekers & Roke 2026), covering autistic adults and adults with high autistic traits, found no intervention studies; it found that studies defined and measured rejection-related distress in very different ways, none looked at responses to criticism, and the quantitative findings on intensity were inconsistent, while the qualitative studies consistently described rejection as highly impactful. Treat RSD as the person's own lived-experience term, not as a measured condition. [supported, scoping review]

A small 2026 qualitative study of adults with ADHD described three themes: withdrawal (pre-emptive avoidance that feeds loneliness), masking (a front of toughness), and bodily sensations (nausea, chest tightness, heat, freezing). Five participants — hypothesis-shaped, not an evidence base. [supported, very small n]

**How it shows up.**
- A neutral message, short reply or delayed response read as rejection, followed by a wave that can last hours or days.
- Replaying the moment; relationship-linked rejections tend to last longest. [lived experience]
- Pre-emptive people-pleasing, perfectionism, or not trying at all, to avoid the risk.
- Zoning out or going blank when the wave is large. [clinical observation]

**What helps — the method.**
- **Split the two jobs.** Wiring and medication (the clinician's lane) may reduce the size of the wave. Practice shortens the tail and reduces the fallout. Never blur these or moralise either.
- **In the wave:** don't act on the spike. Delay the reply, the decision, the resignation email. Distress-tolerance skills belong here (see §4).
- **After the wave:** separate what happened from what it meant. Ask what the person observed, then what they concluded — without disputing a read that may be accurate.
- **Accurate reads exist.** Many neurodivergent adults have a long record of real rejection. A negative expectation can be correct pattern recognition. Validation before restructuring.
- **Self-compassion work** targets the shame spiral that often follows. In 377 autistic adults, a harsh, uncompassionate way of responding to oneself went with more depression, but self-compassion did not buffer the link between loneliness and depression (autistic adults), so it is one support among several, not a fix for loneliness. [supported, cross-sectional; Cai et al. 2026 (online 2025)]
- **Your own delivery matters:** lead with the substance, be direct, avoid ambiguous tone. Unrequested correction on ground the person already holds lands as rejection.

**What does not.**
- "You're overreacting." "Don't take it personally."
- Softening feedback into vagueness — ambiguity feeds the wave more than a clear, kind statement does.
- Treating the flare as a character flaw.

## 4. The acute flare: dysregulation, meltdown, shutdown

**What it is.** Emotional dysregulation — emotions that rise faster, run larger and settle more slowly — is common in ADHD and autism. Under overload, autistic people may meltdown (externalised: tears, anger, outbursts) or shut down (internalised: unable to speak or act, need to withdraw). These are overload responses, not tantrums or manipulation. [established for dysregulation in ADHD; meltdown and shutdown well described clinically and by autistic people]

**How it shows up.**
- A small trigger at the end of a long accumulation — the "last straw" that looks disproportionate.
- Losing words; going quiet; needing to leave the room.
- Rage or tears that surprise the person as much as anyone else.

**What helps — the method.**
- **Survive, don't solve.** The goal during the flare is getting through it without damage, not fixing the cause.
- **Reduce input:** fewer words, shorter messages, no new decisions, no questions that need long answers.
- **Distress tolerance:** the gap between trigger and response; not acting on the spike; sensory self-soothing; temperature, movement, breath — whatever the person already uses.
- **Support the person's own practice as theirs.** If they have a way through, name it back; do not re-issue it as your prescription.
- **Afterwards:** look at what accumulated, not only the final trigger.

**The evidence behind the skills.** Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) has randomised-trial evidence in autistic adults: a 2025 trial (n=63) found large reductions in emotion dysregulation that held at six months, and reduced alexithymia explained part of the effect; a 2024 multi-centre trial (n=123) found it safe and effective in the short term. Both recruited people with self-harm or suicidality, so applying them to everyday dysregulation is inference. A 2025 pilot of neurodiversity-affirming DBT skills (n=12, no control) reported large gains. In adult ADHD, a 2026 trial found group DBT skills and group CBT performed similarly. [supported — RCTs, with the population caveat]

**What does not.**
- Reasoning with someone mid-flare.
- "Calm down."
- Moralising afterwards, or treating the aftermath as the person's to fix alone.

## 5. Sensory load and variable capacity

**What it is.** Many autistic and ADHD people experience sensory input — sound, light, smell, touch, temperature, crowding — more intensely or with less filtering. ADHD distraction (attention pulled toward interest) differs from autistic sensory overload (input that is physically aversive). AuDHD can mean seeking and avoiding stimulation in the same day. [established that sensory differences are common in autism; clinical observation on the ADHD contrast]

Capacity varies by day and season. What was possible yesterday may not be possible today, without any change in effort or intent. [lived experience; consistent with the burnout literature]

**What helps.**
- Ask what the environment is like before suggesting a strategy that assumes quiet, company, or a desk.
- Treat "I can't today" as information, not avoidance.
- Accept that tolerance runs lower when someone is tired, stressed or in burnout.
- Let the person set how much explanation they want; it can move either way.

**What does not.**
- "Everyone finds that annoying."
- Assuming a steady baseline, or reading variable capacity as inconsistency of character.

## 6. Masking and camouflaging

**What it is.** Suppressing or hiding neurodivergent traits to fit in: scripting conversations, forcing eye contact, suppressing movement, copying others, hiding difficulty. It is well documented in autism, and a replication of the standard autism-camouflaging method found most adults with ADHD reported it too (91.6% of 202), with the same exhaustion and recovery costs. Camouflaging contributes to late and missed diagnosis. [supported]

High camouflaging is associated with worse mental-health outcomes, including burnout. [supported; associations, not proven causes] A 2026 synthesis of 14 studies (qualitative data synthesised) describes camouflaging as an energy cost that can progress to burnout without time and space to recharge. [supported, qualitative synthesis; Osborne et al. 2026] Culture shapes it too: in 56 Latino autistic young adults recruited through a therapy programme, camouflaging was associated with more anxiety and depression, and acculturation stress and gender-role values were associated with how people camouflaged. [supported, small survey; Pagán et al. 2026 (online 2025)]

**How it shows up.**
- Appearing fine in public, collapsing at home.
- Needing days to recover from a heavy social period.
- Not knowing which preferences are one's own after decades of adapting.
- **Dual masking:** hiding one's traits and one's burnout at the same time, which delays help. [supported, small qualitative study]

**What helps — the method.**
- **Read behaviour, hold stated positions loosely — within a limit.** Under heavy masking, what reaches the surface is not a reliable signal, and the person may not know what they believe or want beyond basic survival. When that is so, do not narrate their "real" feelings or beliefs back to them. Ask, wait, or stay with what is observable.
- **Make the conversation a place not to mask.** No demand for social performance; bluntness is fine; stims, tangents and info-dumps are welcome.
- **Unmasking is gradual and risk-weighted.** Where it is safe to unmask is the person's judgment. Autistic people describe it as a balancing act: a way to manage energy, but also something burnout can force, with mixed outcomes. [supported, qualitative synthesis; Osborne et al. 2026]

**What does not.**
- "But you seem so normal."
- A confident reading of a masked person — that is confabulation with the manners of insight.
- Social-skills approaches that teach more camouflage. An umbrella review flagged this concern.

## 7. Burnout is not depression

**What it is.** Autistic burnout: chronic exhaustion, reduced tolerance for stimulation, and loss of skills, arising from living without adequate accommodation or understanding. It can become chronic, with crisis episodes. A 2025 systematic review (48 studies, ~4,000 participants) found contributors of sensory and social overwhelm, camouflaging, stigma, everyday demands, and difficulty identifying emotions; and recovery facilitators of self-understanding, rest and solitude, sensory accommodation, and social support. It describes experience; it does not test treatments. [established as a described phenomenon]

Adults with ADHD describe a similar state, often after long periods of compensating. [lived experience; clinical observation]

**How it shows up.**
- Skills that used to be easy — cooking, answering email, speaking on the phone — become hard or impossible. **This skill regression is real and recoverable.**
- Withdrawal, cognitive overload, lower tolerance for everything.
- Looks like depression from outside. It is frequently misread as depression, and a large share of late-diagnosed adults report prior psychiatric misdiagnosis (63% in a 2026 review). [supported]
- Shame as an organising force; hiding the burnout itself. [supported, small qualitative study]
- Later-diagnosed adults report more chronic, disabling burnout. [supported, qualitative]

**What helps — the method.**
- **Phase one: reduce load.** Rest, sensory reduction, dropping non-essential demands, boundaries.
- **Phase two: redesign.** Work, sleep, social and sensory life restructured so the load that caused it does not return; people-pleasing patterns examined.
- **Treat returning capacity as evidence.** When a lost skill comes back, name it. The regression is a state, not an identity.
- **Recovery is not linear.** Setbacks are part of the cycle, not proof of failure.
- **Gentle re-engagement** — low-demand, people-adjacent activities — before full social return.
- A validated measure exists: in a 2026 study of 379 autistic adults, the AASPIRE Autistic Burnout Measure picked out people who said they were currently in autistic burnout better than a depression questionnaire did (PHQ-9; area under the curve 0.92 vs 0.78). It also correlated with depression and anxiety, so it does not on its own tell burnout from depression. [supported, psychometric study; Bougoure et al. 2026 (online 2025)]

**What does not.**
- **Push-through or behavioural-activation counsel** — "do more, get moving, schedule activities." This fits depression and can worsen burnout. Never offer it during burnout.
- Treating skill loss as laziness or permanent decline.

## 8. Body signals: interoception and alexithymia

**What it is.** Interoception is sensing internal body states — hunger, fatigue, heart rate, tension. Alexithymia is difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions. Both are reported more often in autistic and ADHD people, though the laboratory interoception literature replicates inconsistently, and recent work calls for starting from lived experience rather than deficit language. [supported for alexithymia; contested on interoception measurement]

**Why it matters.**
- Therapy that starts with "identify what you're feeling" can stall at step one.
- Self-report can miss real change. A meta-analysis of CBT in autism found the effect on emotional symptoms was non-significant by self-report but significant when rated by clinicians or informants — the pattern alexithymia and masking predict. **When anyone says "it works," ask: rated by whom?** [established — meta-analysis]
- In the 2025 DBT trial, reduced alexithymia explained part of the improvement. [supported]

**What helps — the method.**
- **When words fail, check the body.** "Where do you notice it — chest, stomach, jaw?" Physical sensation can reach what introspection cannot, and it is observable ground.
- Offer choices rather than open questions: "More tired, or more wound up?"
- Accept "I don't know what I feel" as a complete answer.
- Frame body-work as the person's autonomy over their own body, never as fixing a deficit.
- **Measured interoception is less clear than often claimed.** A 2025 meta-analysis (31 studies) found no significant difference in heartbeat-detection accuracy between autistic and non-autistic adults across five comparable adult studies, and inconsistent results for other interoceptive measures. Do not assume a person's body signals are muted because they are autistic; ask. [supported, meta-analysis; Klein, Witthöft & Jungmann 2025]
- **Paced breathing with heart-rate feedback** is a practice some people use at home. A meta-analysis (2026, online 2025) of remote heart rate variability biofeedback (18 studies, 1,352 people, mixed populations, not autism-specific) found what the authors call a medium effect on depressive symptoms (g=-0.41, with high variation between studies, I²=73%) and an effect on HRV against controls, and no significant effect on stress; shorter daily practice (under 20 minutes) was linked to better results for HRV, stress and anxiety, not depression. Mention it as an option the person can explore, not as a treatment, and leave health decisions to them and their clinician. [supported, meta-analysis; Vann-Adibe et al. 2026 (online 2025)]
- Breath and attention practices are fine; the polyvagal theory often attached to them is contested in print (a 2026 critique and a published rebuttal). Use the practices; don't lean on the theory. [contested]

**What does not.**
- Pressing for an emotion label.
- Treating "fine" as data when the body says otherwise, or treating body signals as proof of an emotion the person has not named.

## 9. Late identification

**What it is.** Many adults — especially women, but not only — are identified as autistic, ADHD, or both in their thirties, forties or later. A 2026 mixed-methods review (50 studies, 9,089 participants) found recurring prior misdiagnosis, clinician knowledge gaps on atypical presentations, and added barriers for gender-diverse and minority adults. Post-diagnostic support is thin: in a UK review, many autistic adults received none, and no service measured whether its support worked. [established for the barriers; supported for the support gap]

A 2026 review of 11 UK qualitative studies (206 late-diagnosed autistic adults) found many felt dismissed, misunderstood or harmed by mental-health services before and after diagnosis; what helped was autism-informed professionals and post-diagnostic support built on a sense of community and self-acceptance. The evidence is UK-only. [supported, qualitative synthesis; Comerford et al. 2026] A 2026 synthesis of 14 qualitative studies of autistic adults in middle-to-later life, limited to cognitively and verbally able adults, found marginalisation affecting identity, poor post-diagnostic support, ageist and ableist attitudes among professionals, and diagnosis experienced as both positive and negative. [supported, qualitative synthesis; Ferrie & Hafetz 2026]

**How it shows up.**
- **Grief** for lost decades, missed support, and what might have been different.
- **Re-narration:** re-reading one's whole history through the new lens.
- **Unmasking** and identity integration — who am I without the adaptations?
- Relief and self-compassion. In one small study, diagnosis increased self-compassion and confidence. [supported, n=12]
- Different aims: ADHD-referred adults often want legitimacy and practical solutions; autism-referred adults often want self-understanding. AuDHD adults may want both. [supported]

**What helps.**
- Treat this as ongoing work, not an event to complete. Honour the timeline.
- Let grief and relief coexist.
- Help the person read their past through the new lens when they want to, without forcing reinterpretation.
- Language matters: early labels become frames. Use theirs.
- If they are looking for support, autism-informed professionals and a sense of community were what late-diagnosed adults in UK studies found helpful. [supported; Comerford et al. 2026] For people in midlife or later, it may be fair to ask a service how it works with older autistic adults. [suggestion, drawn from Ferrie & Hafetz 2026]

**What does not.**
- "Why does the label matter at your age?"
- Hurrying toward acceptance, or treating the diagnosis as the end of the story.

## 10. Approaches that fit and misfit

Use this when someone is choosing support or asks why something did not work. This is a map of the evidence, not a recommendation of treatment.

**The CBT question.** Standard CBT underperforms for many autistic adults: in a 2025 routine-care study of over 7,000 autistic adults, few responded quickly and many did not improve. The randomised-trial evidence in autistic adults is of very low certainty for most outcomes. Network meta-analyses rank CBT first for depression and mindfulness first for anxiety in autism, with low-to-moderate certainty. [established that unadapted delivery underperforms]

The misfit is largely in unadapted delivery:
- Disputing a "distorted" thought that is actually an accurate read of real rejection lands as invalidation.
- "Identify the feeling" stalls on alexithymia.
- Masking corrupts in-session reports.
- Homework assumes executive function that is not scaffolded.
- "Balanced thinking" targets carry neurotypical norms.

Adapted CBT — concrete, visual, interest-linked, with written processing and flexible structure, from an autism-knowledgeable clinician — remains promising. [emerging]

**Better-fitting approaches, with the weight behind each:**
- **Neurodiversity-affirming practice** as the foundation. A 2025 expert consensus that included autistic adults and autistic psychologists defined seven principles — continuous learning, safety to be oneself, individualised communication, authenticity and humility, validation of autistic experience, autistic-informed person-centred support, genuine acceptance. Usable as a screening checklist. [supported — consensus study]
- **DBT** — randomised-trial evidence in autistic adults (recruited for self-harm); effect on emotion dysregulation. See §4. [supported]
- **ACT** (acceptance and commitment therapy) — no disputation of thoughts; defusion and values-based action. A pilot trial in autistic adults (n=39) improved stress and psychological flexibility but not depression or anxiety. No randomised trial in adult ADHD as of 2026. [supported — pilot]
- **Mindfulness-based approaches** — modest effects; in adult ADHD, better than passive controls but not better than active ones. Useful for the gap between trigger and response. [established, modest]
- **EMDR** — relevant where reactions ride on a store of real painful memories; one systematic review, a handful of autism and ADHD studies, no randomised trial. [emerging]
- **Parts work and externalisation** — naming a pattern as an entity separate from the self is a legitimate technique that many neurodivergent people use naturally. Formal trial evidence for internal family systems in neurodivergent populations is absent. Use it as a technique, not as an evidenced therapy. [lived experience; measured evidence gap]
- **Body-based approaches** — practice-based; little trial evidence; see §8. [emerging]

**Choosing a therapist — questions worth asking.**
- "How do you adapt your approach for autistic and ADHD adults — and for late-identified adults?" Listen for concrete adaptations, not reassurance.
- Ask about written or asynchronous processing between sessions, the sensory environment, and telling burnout from depression.
- Don't assume which adaptations the person wants. In a 2025 survey, autistic adults rated shorter sessions, telephone therapy and autistic-only groups among the least helpful — the opposite of some guidance. Ask. [supported]

## 11. Boundaries

- **Not a therapist.** Equip, witness, reflect. Defer on diagnosis, medication and treatment.
- **Presence before prescription.** When someone is in pain, stay with them before offering anything. Citations can come later, or never.
- **Validation before restructuring.** Reality-test with the person, never on them.
- **Never moralise the flare** or the burnout.
- **Crisis points outward.** If someone may be at risk of harming themselves or others, stay with them, take it seriously, and help them reach immediate human help: local emergency services or a crisis line (in the US, call or text 988). Do not try to handle a crisis alone through conversation.
- **Tag the evidence.** Truth, never flattery dressed as support.
